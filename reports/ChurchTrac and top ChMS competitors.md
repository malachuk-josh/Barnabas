# ChurchTrac's rivals leave ministry leaders underserved

ChurchTrac is a founder-owned, debt-free all-in-one that sells every module (people, giving, fund accounting, check-in, worship planning, website and member app) for **$29 to $119 a month**, priced only by the number of people tracked. Its roughly 4.8-star reputation rests on fast human support and value. Users fault its dated interface and basic reporting, and the product has a closed API, no staff mobile app and no AI assistant. Its five most relevant rivals are **Planning Center, Tithely Church Management (formerly Breeze), Pushpay ChurchStaq, Realm by ACS Technologies and Rock RMS**. They split into price-led small-church bundles and big-church platforms that set the bar for automation and AI, and all of them are designed around the church office rather than the ministry leader in the field. The market, worth roughly $300–400 million a year, spent 2025–2026 consolidating around payment companies, and the resulting migrations give a new entrant chances to win switchers. None of the three proposed white-space features survives as "not offered anywhere," but each **partly holds** in a narrower, defensible form. No major church management system (ChMS) combines overload and disengagement signals into a volunteer burnout radar. None offers consent-based collaboration between independent churches. None automatically translates staff messages into each member's stored language. Pastor care-load tracking is the cleanest gap of the five candidates. On AI, **2 of the 6 products ship in-product chat** (Planning Center and Rock), **1 ships two-way voice** (Rock), and **none learns from a leader's conversations**, so the user's voice-plus-memory direction is open ground. Claude can power it through the API. Its memory tool maps directly onto one markdown file per user stored in Redis. The Messages API has no audio, though, so voice needs a speech pipeline built around Claude. Memory also needs privacy controls, which neither of the two church products that claim memory documents.

*How to read the evidence:* Capterra, G2, GetApp, Software Advice and several press sites blocked direct reading during the research. Every review rating, review count and user quote below therefore comes from search-engine snippets and is approximate. Capterra, GetApp and Software Advice share one Gartner review pool, so they do not independently confirm each other. Customer counts are vendor claims. The main trend survey (State of Church Technology) is commissioned by Pushpay and fielded by Barna, and about half of its 2026 sample came through Pushpay's own network. Several comparison sites cited here are affiliates or sell competing products: ChurchMemberPro, Church Posting, FlockConnect, One Church Software, CostBench, LearnOfChrist and Aligned. Their claims are labeled where they appear. "Not found" means not found in public documentation, not proven absent.

## A roughly $300–400 million market is consolidating around payment companies

Church management software is a modest, mature market. Search summaries of paywalled analyst listings put global revenue at **$292 million to $398 million in 2025**, growing about **6% a year** ([GII Research listing](https://www.giiresearch.com/report/infi2055787-global-church-management-software-market.html)). Technavio projects **$418.5 million of incremental growth over 2025–2029 at an 8.9% CAGR** ([Technavio](https://www.technavio.com/report/church-management-software-market-industry-analysis)). A $17.23 billion outlier uses a far broader definition and should be ignored ([Spherical Insights](https://www.sphericalinsights.com/reports/church-management-software-market)). The US has between **356,739** congregations (the 2020 U.S. Religion Census, via a secondary source) and about **370,000** (Hartford Institute estimate) ([ReachRight](https://reachrightstudios.com/blog/how-many-churches-in-america/); [Hartford Institute](https://hirr.hartfordinternational.edu/?p=40)). A bottom-up check supports the narrow figures: about 300,000 congregations paying $600–$1,200 a year, the small-church price points vendors publish, comes to about $180–360 million in the US alone. Surveyed churches have already adopted the software. Pushpay's 2025 survey of 1,700+ leaders found **86% use a ChMS** ([Religion Unplugged](https://religionunplugged.com/news/2025/4/30/new-study-reveals-key-tech-trends-shaping-the-us-church)). The 2026 Barna/Pushpay report puts ChMS use at 84%, texting at 80% and a mobile church app at only **54%** ([2026 report PDF, third-party-hosted copy](https://worldtrend.theblendingofthebody.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/barna-pushpay-state-of-church-tech-report-2026-pp.pdf)). Those respondents lean toward tech-using churches, so true adoption among the many tiny congregations is lower. Growth now comes mainly from switching, bundling with payments and upselling apps and AI, not from first-time buyers.

The pattern explains why ChMS software increasingly arrives "free with giving." Tithely bundles church management with free giving. Vanco is folding its processing into Realm. Pushpay is making its giving data the "source of truth" across ChurchStaq. The independents, Planning Center, ChurchTrac and Rock RMS, are now the alternatives for churches wary of investor-driven pricing. ChurchTrac sells this directly: its 2026 "best ChMS" list excludes Tithely and ACS for being investor-owned ([ChurchTrac blog](https://www.churchtrac.com/blog/best-church-management-software)). Vendor scale claims should not be compared head to head. Planning Center's About page claims **78,000+ active churches**, although a directory lists 100,000+. Tithely's "50,000–53,000 churches" includes giving-only accounts. Pushpay claims about 14,000–14,500 churches and parishes, skewed toward the largest churches ([Pushpay About](https://pushpay.com/about-us/)). Two reported deals remain unconfirmed: a claim that Pushpay bought ChurchDesk, which CB Insights lists as acquired by an undisclosed buyer in October 2025 ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/churchdesk/financials)), and conflicting reports about who bought FaithTeams. The churn these deals create matters most for a new entrant. Tithely churches are being migrated to 3.0 in phases they cannot choose. Realm and MinistryPlatform customers face payment-processor changes. Pushpay customers are living through a platform rebuild. Each is a moment when a church reconsiders its tools.

## ChurchTrac sells the whole church office for $29 to $119 a month

ChurchTrac was founded in 2002 by Byron Tedder, who was then on staff at a local church. It is "privately owned and debt free," has "no outside investors," is headquartered in Jacksonville, Florida, and claims **14,000+ churches** (vendor claim) ([ChurchTrac About](https://churchtrac.com/about_us)). It began as Windows desktop software, now retired at ChurchTrac 9, and today is a single web app in which every current plan gets the same features.

### Fifteen modules share one database, but there is no API and no staff app

ChurchTrac covers most small-church needs to real depth. It also shipped heavily in 2025–2026: a native member app, Enhanced Messaging, group chat, a worship app, website templates, a September 2026 visual redesign and an accounting overhaul. The roadmap is aimed at replacing the point tools churches buy alongside a ChMS: Text In Church, GroupMe, Planning Center Services and QuickBooks.

The gaps sit where a ministry leader would feel them most. There is **no native staff or admin app**: administration happens in a desktop browser (Chrome or Safari). The member, check-in and worship apps are the only native apps. The only AI is the bank-statement parser, and ChurchTrac says uploads are deleted after processing and never used to train models ([Importing Transactions](https://www.churchtrac.com/support/accounting/importing-transactions)). Care is assembled from notes, reminders and tasks rather than offered as a care pipeline. Multi-campus churches need separate accounts to keep per-campus books ([Multi Campus](https://www.churchtrac.com/support/getting_started/multi-campus-churches)). ChurchTrac's FAQ says the interface is officially in English and Spanish. One affiliate review calls it English-only, which looks outdated or wrong ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/churchtrac-review/)).

The plan includes a 30-day trial with no card required, but there is no free plan. Church plants get custom quotes. The only meaningful add-on is Enhanced Messaging at $7 a month for 250 credits. Optional one-time services are a Website & App Build from $477 and an Accounting Setup from $399 ([ChurchTrac Pricing](https://www.churchtrac.com/pricing)). Giving runs through Stripe at **2.9% + $0.30 for cards** (3.5% for Amex) or **2.2% + $0.30 at Stripe's nonprofit rate**, and **$0.25 per ACH gift**. ChurchTrac takes no platform fee ([Online Giving Fees](https://www.churchtrac.com/support/giving/online-giving-fees)). ChurchTrac's marketing leads with the 2.2% rate while its FAQ lists 2.9% as the "negotiated" rate; the church's real rate depends on Stripe approving its nonprofit status. This structure is new. A February 2026 affiliate review still described a Free tier for churches of 100 people or fewer, plus Standard from $7 and Plus from $15 ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/churchtrac-review/)). ChurchTrac's own comparison page, "Last Updated April 2026," already shows the $29–$119 table, and SpotSaaS confirms the old feature tiers were replaced ([SpotSaaS](https://www.spotsaas.com/blog/churchtrac-pricing-features-in-2024)). No official announcement was found. For churches under about 100 people, the price went from $0–$15 a month to $29, which opens room for a free or very cheap leader-focused tier. At the top end, $119 a month for unlimited names with every feature is hard to undercut.

### It positions itself as the affordable, accounting-included alternative to Planning Center

ChurchTrac's pitch is "Church software doesn't have to be hard. Or expensive," plus "Small churches matter" and "Built for churches—not shareholders" ([ChurchTrac homepage](https://www.churchtrac.com/); [About](https://churchtrac.com/about_us)). Its comparison hub promises "More Ministry Tools for Half the Cost." It contrasts competitors' "Fund Accounting: Not available" and "Ticket queues & bots" with its own included accounting and "Personal & Proactive" support. It names Planning Center, Tithe.ly, Breeze, QuickBooks, Aplos, Realm, Subsplash, PowerChurch, Church Windows and spreadsheets as alternatives ([Comparison hub](https://www.churchtrac.com/comparison)). Its Planning Center page claims Planning Center costs "three times more." The page's own table shows $59 vs $190 a month at 250 people and $119 vs "$1,000+" for unlimited, ratios of 2.7x to 8.4x that contradict the "three times" headline (vendor claim). ChurchTrac also concedes Planning Center suits churches "that rely on multiple integrations" or need "modular tools for a large multi-campus setup" ([ChurchTrac vs Planning Center](https://www.churchtrac.com/comparisons/churchtrac-vs-planningcenter)). Estimates of the right church size vary by who is writing. An affiliate review says the best fit is US churches under 250 members. A competitor says up to about 2,000. ChurchTrac's own list says "under 2,000 members." One church reportedly outgrew it at about 400 members and moved to Planning Center (affiliate anecdote) ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/churchtrac-review/); [FlockConnect, competitor](https://flockconnect.com/blog/churchtrac-vs-planning-center-2026); [ChurchTrac blog](https://www.churchtrac.com/blog/best-church-management-software)). Giving supports US cards and ACH only, which limits international appeal.

### Users praise its support and value and fault the interface and reports

On Capterra ChurchTrac scores about **4.8/5 from ~864 reviews**, with 736 of them five-star (about 85%). GetApp shows ease of use 4.7, customer support 4.9 and 90% would recommend, though it draws on the same Gartner review pool ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/125787/ChurchTrac/reviews/); [GetApp](https://www.getapp.ca/reviews/105371/churchtrac); search snippets). That is the second-largest review base in the category, behind Planning Center's ~1,139, which suggests active review solicitation. G2 has only 12 reviews (4.8). The homepage awards are all dated 2024. Praise centers on support ("never an issue that isn't resolved the very day I send in an inquiry") and value ("so affordable for a small church our size"). Complaints center on the interface: it "feels outdated and could benefit from modernization," and it is "not very modern or user friendly." Reviewers also say the "reporting engine is very basic," that "managing fine-grained permissions and hierarchical church organization structures can be complex," and that they want to use it on a phone and pay through processors other than Stripe ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/churchtrac/reviews); [Capterra UK](https://www.capterra.co.uk/reviews/125787/churchtrac); search snippets). A competitor claims churches leave because they "outgrew a basic setup" and wanted deeper reports, a branded app, an API and multi-campus support. Treat that as low-reliability marketing ([One Church Software, competitor](https://onechurchsoftware.com/one-church-software-vs-churchtrac/)). The pattern is typical of a bootstrapped, long-lived product: excellent service and price, with a modern interface, mobile experience and analytics left behind. A third-party tracker estimates the iOS member app at about 1,000 monthly active users, which suggests limited member engagement ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/6737914083)).

Read the matrix as three dividing lines. **Accounting** separates ChurchTrac and Realm from everyone else. On those terms, ChurchTrac is the broadest cheap bundle but the least open and least mobile for staff.

### How each rival stacks up against ChurchTrac

**Planning Center** is the volume leader. It was founded in 2006, is based in Carlsbad, has 200+ staff and has never taken investor money ([Planning Center About](https://www.planningcenter.com/about)). Its modular pricing is the opposite of ChurchTrac's. People and the Church Center member app are free, and each paid module climbs the same ladder, **$15 → $32 → $69 → $115 → $179 → $239 a month**, by its own usage metric. Card giving costs 2.15% + $0.30 and ACH $0.30 ([Planning Center Pricing](https://www.planningcenter.com/pricing)). A calculation from those tiers puts a 150-attendee church using six modules at about $175 a month, roughly three times ChurchTrac's $59 for 250 names. Very small churches using one or two modules can pay less than ChurchTrac. Planning Center's 2026 roadmap went straight at its fragmentation complaint: a free staff app (April 2026), unified help and search, expanded workflow automation, and **Ask AI** across every product (October 2026) ([Planning Center app](https://www.planningcenter.com/blog/2026/04/planning-center-app); [changelog](https://www.planningcenter.com/changelog)). Its weaknesses match ChurchTrac's pitch: no accounting, no full website, and a Reddit complaint that "the sliding scale fee on every various module just seems like things add up," quoted on a rival vendor's site ([Church Posting, sells a competing product](https://churchposting.com/resources/software/planning-center-vs-breeze-vs-churchtrac/)). It also offers no languages other than English: "There are no immediate plans to support Planning Center in other languages" ([Planning Center FAQ](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/136588-planning-center-faq.html)).

**Tithely Church Management** is Breeze under a new name. Breeze was acquired in 2021 and renamed on November 11, 2025, with "no cost increase tied to the transition" ([Tithely blog](https://get.tithe.ly/blog/breezechms-is-integrated-into-tithely-church-management-heres-what-you-need-to-know)). At **$72 a month flat** with unlimited contacts, it undercuts ChurchTrac for any church above 250 names ($79 to $119 at ChurchTrac). Its $119 All Access bundle adds a custom app, website and worship planning ([Tithely Pricing](https://get.tithe.ly/pricing)). Reviewers love its simplicity: "every one of my staff was able to use Breeze from the very start" ([Capterra, search snippet](https://www.capterra.com/p/132513/Breeze-ChMS/reviews/)). It has no fund accounting. Its QuickBooks Desktop export "does not contain what fund the contributions were designated towards," and Breeze support calls the extension "not best practice" ([Breeze support via search](https://support.breezechms.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001324193)). Giving fees are its biggest economic weakness against ChurchTrac. A $500 ACH gift costs **$5.30 at Tithely** (1% + $0.30) against **$0.25 through ChurchTrac's Stripe**. Reviewers also cite slow support at Christmas and Easter and bugs after updates during the 3.0 migration ([Solomon Wealth Code](https://www.solomonwealthcode.com/blog/tithely-review); [Software Advice, search snippet](https://www.softwareadvice.com/church/breeze-chms-profile/reviews/)).

**Pushpay ChurchStaq** sits above ChurchTrac's market. It is quote-only and sold on one- to three-year contracts ([Pushpay Pricing](https://pushpay.com/pricing/)). Affiliate guides say it fits churches of 500+ attendance and is "not recommended" under 300 members ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/ccb-church-community-builder-review/)). It is the rival most aimed at ministry leaders. It acquired Nurture.io for pastoral-care task lists. In September 2026 it shipped AI Profile Summaries, which combine attendance, groups, serving, notes and giving into a pastoral briefing "prepared in less than 30 seconds." It also offers a Lead app for leaders, a ChMS in five languages, and Microsoft Entra and Okta single sign-on ([Pushpay releases](https://pushpay.com/product/releases/)). Its weaknesses are cost, contracts and age. It has the lowest Capterra sub-scores in this set (support 3.8, value 3.6) ([Capterra AU, search snippet](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/142580/pushpay)). A Reddit complaint, quoted secondhand on affiliate review pages and not verified at the source, says its contracts are "extremely difficult and expensive to leave" ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/pushpay-review/)). There is no worship planning, so one user quoted by an affiliate site says "we're paying for CCB AND Planning Center" ([ChurchMemberPro, affiliate](https://churchmemberpro.com/blog/ccb-church-community-builder-review/)).

**Realm** is the rival that beats ChurchTrac on accounting. Its ledger posts contribution batches as deposits and supports both cash and accrual methods. It also offers a segment-based chart of accounts, accounts payable with check runs, payroll and a change-log audit trail. ChurchTrac's register-style books lack payroll, invoices and accrual ([Realm Accounting Brief](https://acstechnologies.com/realm/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2025/02/Realm-Accounting-Brief.pdf)). It also has a dedicated leader app, Shepherd, alongside the Connect member app. Only the entry tier has a published price: **Inform at $49 a month**. Connect and Multiply are quote-only, and accounting is described as an add-on ([Realm Inform store](https://buy.acst.com/realm-inform/); [ACS Realm](https://www.acstechnologies.com/realm/)). ACS says plainly, "We intentionally built Realm to serve the needs of administrators." Its review base is dated, with most reviews from 2018–2021. A 2024 review reports "Terrible problems with their online giving platform." It has announced no AI, and February 2026 release notes only hint at "an upcoming improved mobile experience" ([Capterra, search snippet](https://www.capterra.com/p/146265/Realm/reviews/?page=8); [Realm release notes](https://help.acst.com/en/realm/whats-new/2026/february-4-2026)).

**Rock RMS** is free, open-source software from an Arizona nonprofit. Churches self-host it on Windows, IIS and SQL Server or pay for managed hosting from **$419 to $4,419 a month** ([Rock hosting](https://www.rockrms.com/hosting); [Rock Cloud pricing](https://www.rockcloud.com/pricing)). It is the most powerful product here: a visual workflow engine, built-in website CMS, SQL reporting, a plugin marketplace and a fast release cadence (v17 April 2025, v18 December 2025, v19 June 2026, v20 in alpha September 2026) ([Rock release timeline](https://www.rockrms.com/release-timeline)). It is also the only product in the set with a shipped AI voice agent. Reviewers warn that it "requires someone who knows the ins-and-outs of databases" ([Capterra, search snippet](https://www.capterra.com/p/141654/Rock-RMS/reviews/?page=5)). Affiliate sources cite partner implementations of $5,000–$20,000 and setups of two to six months ([LearnOfChrist, affiliate](https://learnofchrist.com/resources/rock-rms)). Its 4.9 rating likely reflects technical admins who chose it deliberately, not ministry leaders' experience. There is no official customer count. ChurchTrac claims Rock "powers 10 of the 12 largest churches," a competitor's claim ([ChurchTrac blog](https://www.churchtrac.com/blog/best-church-management-software)).

## Every incumbent is built for the office, which leaves the ministry leader open

The clearest opening is the user the incumbents treat as secondary. ChurchTrac has no staff app at all. Planning Center launched its first unified staff app only in April 2026. Realm says it was built for administrators, and Rock and MinistryPlatform need technical staff to build good leader experiences. Youth pastors, small-group leaders, worship leaders and care teams work on their phones between people, yet the products are organized around the database and the treasurer. Survey data points the same way. Communication is the top challenge leaders hope technology will solve (51%) ([Religion Unplugged](https://religionunplugged.com/news/2025/4/30/new-study-reveals-key-tech-trends-shaping-the-us-church)). Ease of use leads buying decisions, ahead of price (66%), reputation (61%) and data security (59%), with feature depth and integrations outside the top four ([Pushpay blog](https://pushpay.com/?p=19291)). Pushpay's own framing of the 2026 survey describes the small-church reality: at about 150 members, one person carries giving reports, visitor follow-up and volunteer scheduling on top of preaching and counseling, and the core question is "can I have this working by Sunday?" (vendor framing).

The specific gaps are concrete. ChurchTrac's care loop is manual: run the absentee report, then send a text. ChurchTrac users want better reports, and Planning Center reviewers describe a reporting "learning curve." Meanwhile, natural-language questions over church data are becoming table stakes (Planning Center Ask AI, MinistryPlatform AI, Subsplash Trends AI). The US-centric leaders (Planning Center, Tithely, Realm, ChurchTrac) offer no WhatsApp, and Planning Center offers no other languages. ChurchTrac's $29 floor priced out micro-churches that used to pay nothing. AI governance is nearly absent: **only 5% of churches have AI guidelines** while **83% have privacy concerns** ([Barna](https://www.barna.com/research/church-leaders-ai-usage-concerns/)).

The incumbents' moves also create a delivery path. In 2026 Planning Center (MCP across every product, August), MinistryPlatform (MP-MCP, generally available in August) and Rock (MCP agents) opened their data to outside AI tools, and Breeze offers a REST API. A ministry-leader app can therefore sit **on top of** an existing ChMS rather than replace it, which removes the "working by Sunday" switching fear. ChurchTrac is the exception. Its closed API means its churches can be served only through CSV exports or a standalone data model, so ChurchTrac is both the price benchmark and the hardest platform to build on top of.

Two threats temper these openings. Pushpay is aiming its Nurture and AI Profile Summaries work squarely at pastoral leaders, and Planning Center ships quickly and owns the largest base. A new entrant has to win on the leader's daily experience (mobile, voice, memory, proactive nudges) and on trust, not on database breadth.

## Five features worth building, with honest gap verdicts

The user has decided to build these features. The verdicts below say how much of each is genuinely new, which shapes how to position it and what the product must do to stand apart.

|---|---|---|---|
| Volunteer burnout radar | **Partly holds** | Church.IQ (0–10 load score), Vitals (on Planning Center data), Holy Insights, Pushpay Nurture (engagement alerts), Planning Center Services caps, Churchteams reports | Fuse overload with disengagement: cross-ministry load, consecutive weeks, decline rates and drops in the volunteer's own group and worship attendance, explained, with a suggested action sent to the right leader |
| Consent-based collaboration between independent churches | **Partly holds** | CharityTracker, Meet the Need, CarePortal, Crisis Cleanup, JustServe, Gloo Church Metrics Groups, diocesan editions of Pushpay and MinistryPlatform | Peer-to-peer sharing chosen per data type, with person-level consent, tied to each church's own people, volunteer and event data, covering several kinds of joint work, and interoperating with CharityTracker and CarePortal |

### Volunteer burnout radar: partly holds, because small entrants already score load

No major ChMS offers a volunteer burnout risk score. That covers Planning Center, Pushpay, Tithely, Rock, Realm, MinistryPlatform, Subsplash, ChurchTrac, Churchteams and ChMeetings. The strict claim "not offered in the marketplace" fails, though. **Church.IQ** markets "AI burnout detection (0–10 score)," a "Weighted score based on departments (up to +4), roster frequency (up to +3), leadership load (+2), and volume (+1)," with HIGH ≥ 6 and WATCH ≥ 3 bands, and calls itself "A World First" ([Church.IQ](https://www.church-iq.com/)). **Vitals**, an analytics layer that syncs Planning Center data, markets "burnout risk" and "burnout detection" with undisclosed inputs and weekly summary reports only ([Vitals](https://vitals.church/church-analytics-software)). **Holy Insights** lists "burnout risk" under volunteer intelligence in its $80-a-month tier ([Holy Insights](https://www.holyinsights.org/)). The large vendors offer rules instead of scores. Planning Center volunteers can cap how often they serve, and schedulers who exceed the cap get a "preference warning" ([Planning Center help](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/set-your-scheduling-preferences.html)). Churchteams runs scheduled reports such as "Who hasn't served in the last 60 days and might be drifting or approaching burnout?" ([Churchteams](https://go.churchteams.com/preventing-church-volunteer-burnout/)). Pushpay's Nurture alerts staff when "someone's engagement shifts across multiple areas" ([Pushpay Nurture](https://pushpay.com/integrations/nurture/)). TouchPoint's weekly engagement score treats heavy serving as *more* engagement, the opposite of a burnout signal ([TouchPoint](https://www.touchpointsoftware.com/features/engagement/)). The market splits into an overload camp and a disengagement camp, and no product found merges them.

The need is well documented. Gallup found the share of Americans volunteering for a religious organization fell from 44% in 2017 to 35% in 2021 ([Christianity Today](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2022/01/church-ministry-volunteer-gallup-survey-lifeway-wr)). Hartford's EPIC study found volunteering fell from about 40% of membership in early 2020 to about 20% in March 2022. By 2023 it had recovered only to about 35% in majority-white and multiracial congregations. These figures come from search summaries, because the primary pages were blocked ([SSRC Intersections](https://intersections.ssrc.org/2024/01/11/pandemic-boosted-resilience-hurt-financial-health-of-black-churches-says-report/?pdf=1855)). Lifeway names over-reliance as a cause: leaders lean on specific volunteers "so much so that the volunteers burn out and quit serving." It advises checking whether the same people serve in every ministry area, which is exactly the cross-ministry signal this feature targets (search summary) ([Lifeway Research](https://lifewayresearch.com/2019/04/03/5-signs-of-burnout-in-your-volunteers/)).

Our version must merge the two camps. It should count true cross-module load: Sunday teams, children's check-in rosters, group leadership and event volunteering. It should add consecutive weeks served and the trend in declined requests; Rock's scheduler already stores accepted, declined and pending responses ([Rock documentation](https://community.rockrms.com/documentation/bookcontent/7/361)). It should treat a drop in the volunteer's *own* small-group or worship attendance as a burnout warning rather than ordinary disengagement. It should also weigh appreciation and check-in recency, since Churchteams argues that "Volunteer burnout usually isn't caused by too much serving. It's often caused by feeling unseen." Each alert should explain its drivers and suggest an action, for example "check in with Sarah: nine straight weeks across three teams, and she has skipped her group twice." It should never call itself a "world first," and it should avoid presenting unvalidated rules of thumb (such as twice a month) as science. Pushpay is the fastest likely follower. It already has Nurture's multi-signal alerts and "individual engagement summaries" on its AI roadmap ([Pushpay blog](https://pushpay.com/blog/innovation-that-sparks-ministry-action-pushpays-intentional-approach-to-ai)).

### Consent-based collaboration between churches: partly holds, because benevolence networks already exist

Mainstream ChMS products stop at the walls of one church. Planning Center says data can't be shared or synced between accounts and that accounts "cannot be merged" (search snippet) ([Planning Center help](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/136812-multiple-accounts-or-multiple-campuses-.html)). Diocesan editions go further but rely on hierarchy, not consent. ParishStaq for Diocese centralizes reporting across parishes ([Pushpay](https://pushpay.com/?p=14788)), and MinistryPlatform lets "leaders over multiple parishes" view groups of parishes ([ACST](https://www.acstechnologies.com/ministryplatform/catholic/)). Gloo's Church Metrics Groups shares only aggregate totals within invitation-only networks ([Church Metrics](https://support.churchmetrics.com/article/7466sry7bu-what-is-church-metrics-groups)). The broad claim fails, however, because shared work across churches is a mature category outside the ChMS. **CharityTracker** has run city benevolence networks since about 2007. It claims "thousands of churches in over 2,500 cities" (vendor claim) and charges $20–$60 per user per month. Its notes and assistance records can be shared with the network or kept private ([CharityTracker](https://churches.charitytracker.net/); [demo slides](https://s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/misc-hosted-files/resources/CharityTracker++Walk+Thru+Power+Point+Demo+Slides.pdf)). Client profiles, though, are visible network-wide by default, and no ChMS integration was found ([G2, search snippet](https://www.g2.com/products/CharityTracker/reviews)). **Meet the Need** is free and still maintained in 2026. It combines shared case management with volunteer and city-wide event tools, but it publishes no usage figures or consent model ([Meet the Need](https://meettheneed.org/)). **CarePortal** pushes caseworker-vetted child-welfare needs to churches within 10–15 miles ([CarePortal](https://www.careportal.org/how-careportal-works)). **Crisis Cleanup** and **JustServe** (12,000–20,000+ organizations) cover disaster work orders and volunteer listings ([Iowa VOAD](https://www.iavoad.org/resources/crisis-cleanup/); [JustServe](https://www.justserve.org/content/about)). An earlier lead about a "State of Our City" pilot could not be verified and should be dropped.

Demand is real, but recent hard numbers are thin. Love INC alone reports 150+ affiliates in 30 states representing 9,000+ churches (search summary) ([Love INC](https://www.loveinc.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Ambassador_AboutLoveINC_Final.pdf)). One Tyler, Texas serve effort drew 56 churches (search snippet) ([Baptist Standard](https://baptiststandard.com/news/texas/tyler-ministry-delivers-over-100-ac-units-to-families/)). An older Leadership Network survey, reported secondhand, found 87% of externally focused churches partner with other churches on food programs. Only 1% named doctrine as a major obstacle; the barriers were initiation, leadership, shared vision and resources ([Krista Petty blog](https://kristapetty.blogspot.com/2008/06/externally-focused-churches.html)). Software can lower the resource and initiation barriers but cannot create shared vision.

Our version must be **inside the church's own system**, so people, volunteers and events need no second entry. Each church should choose, per data type and peer to peer, what to share, and individuals should consent before their contact details or volunteer availability leave their home church. It should cover several kinds of joint work at once: shared serve opportunities, benevolence de-duplication, joint events and registration, referrals and cross-church groups. It should connect to CharityTracker and CarePortal rather than try to replace free or entrenched incumbents. It should launch through existing conveners such as Love INC affiliates, city movements and associations, since churches rarely organize themselves. Gloo's data practices have drawn privacy criticism ([Christian Post](https://christianpost.com/news/churches-using-big-data-to-target-new-members.html)), which makes consent-first positioning a real point of difference. Incumbents' one-church-per-account architecture also makes this a structural change for them rather than a quick feature.

### Fully multilingual member experience: partly holds, because only interfaces and system text are translated

The earlier framing, that "translation exists only for sermons," is wrong. Several products already ship pieces of a multilingual experience:

| Piece | Who ships it | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Translated interface | Pushpay's ChMS and LEAD app are "now available in 5 languages - English, Spanish, Vietnamese, Korean and Chinese" ([Pushpay](https://pushpay.com/solutions/catholic/multilingual)). ChMeetings lets "each person use ChMeetings in their preferred language," in about 23 languages ([ChMeetings](https://chmeetings.com/all-features)). TouchPoint offers Spanish ([TouchPoint](https://www.touchpointsoftware.com/features/translations/)). Church Online Platform offers 63 viewer languages ([Church Online](https://support.online.church/l/en/article/gobpovgbmk-localization)). ChurchTrac offers Spanish. | Fixed button and menu text; MinistryPlatform warns that record data "appears in the language it was entered in" ([MinistryPlatform KB](https://kb.ministryplatform.com/kb/widgets/user-widgets/language-locale)) |
| Stored language on the member record | Flocknote, ParishSoft, and ChurchCRM (a change merged to a feature branch on Oct 31, 2026) ([Flocknote](https://help.flocknote.com/article/226-using-flocknote-in-spanish-speaking-communities); [Diocese of Fort Wayne–South Bend](https://diocesefwsb.org/how-to-choose-the-spanish-language-in-parishsoft); [ChurchCRM](https://github.com/ChurchCRM/CRM/pull/10858)) | Every one switches system text or selects a version written by hand. Flocknote: "the English notes you write will need to be translated on your end" |

Planning Center has "no immediate plans to support Planning Center in other languages" ([Planning Center FAQ](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/136588-planning-center-faq.html)). Breeze support suggests using Chrome's page translation (search snippet) ([Breeze support](https://support.breezechms.com/hc/en-us/articles/360036848893-Can-I-change-the-language-to-Spanish)). What no product does is the full job: store each member's language, automatically translate whatever staff write for each recipient, and let staff review it before sending, across every channel.

The addressable need is large. About **74.1 million US residents (23%)** aged 5 and over speak a language other than English at home ([USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/answers/how-many-people-speak-a-language-other-than-english-at-home/country/united-states/)). **4,479 Catholic parishes (28%)** offer Spanish Masses ([National Catholic Register](https://www.ncregister.com/cna/survey-shows-28-of-u-s-dioceses-have-spanish-language-masses)). In Lifeway's study of Hispanic Protestant churches, 53% worship only in Spanish and 22% are bilingual, and 58% of their congregants are first-generation immigrants ([ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/news/443383-u-s-hispanic-protestant-landscape-full-of-growing-vibrant-churches-lifeway.html)). There were about 4,454 Korean churches in the US as of 2018 ([Korea Daily](https://en.koreadaily.com/?p=14613)). Multiracial congregations doubled from 12% in 2000 to 25% in 2020 ([ChurchLeadership.com](https://www.churchleadership.com/leading-ideas/diverse-congregations-are-stronger-congregations/)). The 2026 Barna/Pushpay report names multilingual tools among leaders' top priorities for the next three years. Pushpay sells multilingual tools and supplied half the sample, so treat that ranking with care ([MinistryWatch](https://ministrywatch.com/the-new-church-tech-divide-is-missional-not-digital/)). Spanish-only worship is declining across generations, which means mixed-language households and argues for a per-person setting rather than a per-church one.

Our version must store a per-member **written-communication language**, including a "both" option. It must not infer language from names, because, as one diocese warns, "do not assume that because a person is fluent in Spanish, they want their written communication in Spanish." It must translate each outgoing email, text, WhatsApp message, push notification, form, giving page and care follow-up per recipient. It should hold translations in a review queue, which no product offers, and send WhatsApp as a true broadcast channel. ChMeetings is the biggest risk of the gap closing, because it is one integration step away. Pushpay comes second, and Gloo holds the language technology through XRI Global but has no member-language model yet.

### Backup: pastor and staff care-load tracking holds cleanly

This is the only candidate whose gap holds without qualification. No product found combines each pastor's care caseload by type (hospital visits, counseling, crisis calls) with hours, days off and sabbath into a burnout signal that can reach elders. The components exist separately. Planning Center's Workflow Assignees tab lets admins "find out who has the heaviest workload," but it counts tasks only ([Planning Center changelog](https://planningcenter.com/changelog/people/new-workflow-assignees-tab)). Church Dashboards with CareNote lists an undefined "Care team workload" for $50 a month per campus and was "launching right now" in 2026 ([Church Dashboards](https://www.churchdashboards.app/)). Notebird tracks care cases from $12 per user per month ([Capterra](https://capterra.com/p/10019047/Notebird/)). Chemistry Staffing sells a periodic staff-health assessment ($995 for the 2024 report) ([Chemistry Staffing](https://www.chemistrystaffing.com/health-assessment/)). The Flourishing in Ministry assessment draws on more than 20,000 clergy surveys ([CRCNA](https://www.crcna.org/pcr/pastors/flourishing-ministry-study)). Microsoft Viva Insights measures after-hours work ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/viva/insights/quiet-time-in-viva-insights)). Every church burnout-risk tool found (Holy Insights, Vitals) targets volunteers, not pastors.

Demand has softened since 2022 but remains structural. Barna's January 2026 data shows 24% of senior pastors seriously considered leaving full-time ministry, down from about 42% in 2022, which Barna calls "stabilization, not full recovery" ([Barna](https://www.barna.com/trends/pastors-quitting-ministry-barna-data/)). Lifeway's 2025 study found 57% find the role "frequently overwhelming," 34% feel isolated, 67% feel on call 24 hours a day, and only **78% rest at least one day a week, down from 85% in 2015**. It also found 22% of predecessors left because of burnout, though actual attrition is only about 1.2% a year ([Christianity Today](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/06/pastor-leave-church-rate-ministry-burnout-survey)). Secondary coverage of Barna/Gloo's May 2026 study reports 52% of pastors get no professional support ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260505827606/en/Pastors-Emotional-Resilience-Rises-as-Job-Satisfaction-Declines-New-Research-Finds)). An ARDA analysis found life satisfaction barely varies with hours worked (69% under 20 hours vs 71% at 50+), so hours alone are a poor proxy (search summary) ([ARDA](https://thearda.com/categories/ahead-of-the-trend/are-members-of-the-clergy-miserable)).

Our version must let the **pastor own the data** and share only aggregates with the board unless the pastor chooses otherwise; monitoring in the style of Viva Insights would destroy trust. It should weight the kind and intensity of care (crisis and counseling more than routine visits), isolation and missed rest more than raw hours. It should fill in automatically from care logs, and the voice assistant is a natural capture point: a pastor saying "just left the hospital with the Lees" logs a care event without a form. No willingness-to-pay data exists, and elders and boards are likely buyers who are not the users. Church Dashboards is the likeliest fast follower and deserves a demo.

### Backup: a privacy-governed AI assistant fails as stated but holds in a narrower form

The core controls already ship at two church platforms. MinistryPlatform AI lets churches "Turn any table — or individual field — off entirely" ("Pastoral notes too sensitive? Lock the whole table"). It logs "Every query and every data point," inspects responses with a PII Guard, and is generally available ([MinistryPlatform AI](https://ai.ministryplatform.com/)). Its help page says permissions are "enforced by the Platform itself — not by the AI" and lists Claude as the "Recommended" client ([ACST help](https://help.acst.com/en/ministryplatform/help-topics/integrations/mp-mcp-ai-for-ministryplatform)). TouchPoint's MCP server redacts personal information by default, writes "Every tool call" to an audit log, is opt-in, and keeps write actions off by default ([TouchPoint](https://www.touchpointsoftware.com/features/ai-church-management-software/)). Rock puts security on agents, skills and tools, and uses AI to remove last names from prayer requests ([Rock developer docs](https://community.rockrms.com/developer/ai-agents); [Rock prayer docs](https://community.rockrms.com/documentation/engagement/prayer/prayer-requests/administer-prayer-requests)). Planning Center lets admins limit who connects its MCP and hard-codes "security codes, medical notes, and emergency contacts" out of Check-Ins AI responses. However, Ask AI cannot be turned off ("Not right now"), and no query audit log is documented ([Planning Center AI](https://www.planningcenter.com/ai); [Check-Ins changelog](https://www.planningcenter.com/changelog/picolabs/new-ask-your-ai-tools-about-check-ins-attendance-and-headcounts)).

What remains open is narrower. No vendor turns a church's *own written AI policy* into enforced rules; Pushpay's AI Policy Generator produces only a document ([Pushpay AI Policy](https://pushpay.com/ai-policy/)). Every shipped control works on structure (tables, fields, roles). None detects counseling, crisis, prayer or minors' content inside free-text notes. Small and mid-size churches on Planning Center, Pushpay, Tithely and ChurchTrac have no documented field-level AI controls or query audit logs. Demand is clear: 83% of leaders are concerned about data privacy, 64% say an AI policy matters, and only 5% have one ([Barna](https://www.barna.com/research/church-leaders-ai-usage-concerns/)). For this product, this candidate should not stand alone. It is **the governance layer of the user's own memory assistant**: the same rules that decide what the bot may remember about a congregant are the church's AI policy, enforced in code.

## Claude can supply the brain and the memory, but voice needs a pipeline around it

### The short answer: yes, with voice and memory storage built around the Messages API

A Claude-powered chat feature can be connected through the API to deliver this product, with one important qualification. The Claude Messages API accepts text, images and PDFs as input and returns text. Anthropic's Features overview lists **no native audio input or speech output** ([Anthropic Features overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/overview)). Claude can therefore be the conversational brain and the memory manager, but not the ears and mouth. Claude's consumer apps do have a beta voice mode and their own memory ([Claude Help Center: voice](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11101966); [Claude Help Center: memory](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273)), but those are app features. A product built on the API assembles the equivalents itself. "Learning" also needs a precise definition: **Claude's memory is stored notes it reads and writes, not retraining of the model.** The bot gets better at serving a leader because its notes about that leader improve, while the model itself stays the same.

| Layer | Job | Build guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Speech-to-text | Streaming transcription with end-of-turn detection, so the bot knows when the leader has finished speaking | Pick an engine that streams partial results; the turn-end signal starts the Claude call |
| Orchestration | Hosts the real-time loop, audio transport and barge-in | Open-source frameworks such as LiveKit Agents and Pipecat can host this pipeline (example options, not endorsements) |
| Claude | Conversation, reasoning, tool use | Streaming on; prompt caching for the stable system prompt and memory prefix; effort tuned for speed |
| Memory | Per-leader notes that persist across calls | Claude's memory tool (`memory_20250818`) backed by one markdown file per leader in Redis |
| Text-to-speech | Speaks the reply as it streams | Send text to speech one sentence at a time, so the first sentence plays while Claude is still writing |
| Barge-in | Lets the leader interrupt | Stop playback the moment the leader starts speaking, then hand the new audio to speech-to-text |

### Voice: a cascade that streams at every stage

Because the API has no audio ([Anthropic Features overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/overview)), two-way voice on Claude is a cascade: **streaming speech-to-text with end-of-turn detection → Claude with streaming on → sentence-by-sentence streaming text-to-speech**, plus barge-in that stops playback when the user starts speaking. This differs from Rock's Voice Agent, which opens a speech-to-speech session with a realtime model provider. The cascade adds hops, so every stage has to stream for the conversation to feel natural. In return, every turn produces a clean text transcript. That transcript is what the memory layer reads and writes, and what an audit log records, which matters for pastoral data. Voice is also where sensitive details most easily slip in. A leader dictating notes in the car after a hospital visit will say things they would never type, so the privacy modes described below must apply to voice exactly as they do to text.

### Memory: one markdown file per leader in Redis fits the memory tool's design

Claude's memory tool, `memory_20250818`, is **client-side**. Claude requests file operations (view, create, str_replace, insert, delete, rename) under a `/memories` path, and the application runs them against storage it controls. Anthropic's documentation says that path maps onto "real storage, such as a per-user directory or keys in a database," so **a per-user Redis key holding markdown fits the design directly** ([Anthropic memory tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)). When the tool is enabled, Claude automatically checks its memory directory before starting a task and records what it learns. SDK helpers mean the application supplies only the storage layer: BetaAbstractMemoryTool in Python and C#, betaMemoryTool in TypeScript, and BetaMemoryToolHandler in Java. The application must enforce **path-traversal protection and per-user isolation**, and the documentation recommends **size caps and memory expiration**. The tool is available on all Claude 4 and later models. It is listed as eligible for zero data retention and HIPAA readiness, because storage is client-side and the application controls retention. It pairs with compaction and context editing for long conversations ([Anthropic memory tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)).

In practice the Redis handler should derive the key namespace from the authenticated session, for example a church ID plus a leader ID, and **never from the path Claude requests**. It should reject any path that leaves `/memories` or contains traversal sequences, and enforce a byte cap per file and per leader. Splitting memory into a small core file (role, ministries, preferences, standing commitments, open follow-ups) and separate topic files keeps the always-loaded portion short. Separate scopes for areas such as pastoral care, sermon preparation and administration mirror the mainstream pattern: Claude's consumer app isolates memory per project ([Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273)). Because Redis is an in-memory store, it should be configured for persistence, backups and encryption, since it becomes the system of record for what a pastor's assistant knows. The mainstream assistants also separate **explicit memories** ("remember that I lead the youth team") from **memories inferred** from conversation. Users should be able to control each tier separately.

### Latency: load memory up front, stream everything, write afterward

Each memory tool call is an extra round trip before Claude speaks, which is fatal in voice if it happens before the first word. The recommended pattern is a **hybrid**. Load the leader's core memory markdown from Redis into the system prompt when the call starts, then write memory updates during the call or in a background step after the call ends. Use streaming. Use prompt caching so the stable system prompt and memory prefix are reused across turns, and **pre-warm the cache when a call opens** so the first answer does not pay the full cost. Fill any unavoidable tool-call gap (a ChMS lookup, a deeper memory read) with a short spoken holding phrase such as "Let me check Sunday's roster." Tune the effort setting: start Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort for conversation and measure **time to first spoken word** as the key metric. Fast mode on Opus (up to 2.5x output speed at premium pricing) and smaller Claude models are options to test against that metric; the choice is the user's ([Anthropic Features overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/overview)). For live church data, Planning Center, MinistryPlatform and Rock now publish MCP servers that list Claude as a supported client. How those servers connect through the Messages API, and what each lookup adds to latency, should be confirmed in Anthropic's documentation during design.

### Privacy: the rules about congregants are the core design decision

The privacy design will decide whether churches trust the product. Every leader's memory must be isolated from every other leader's, including on the same church staff. Leaders must be able to **view, edit and delete** what the bot remembers, including by asking "what do you know about me?" and "forget that." The bot must **never store secrets** such as passwords, account numbers or door codes. Most important, the app needs **explicit rules for what may be remembered about the congregants a leader cares for.** A sensible default is to store a ChMS person reference and a minimal follow-up ("check on him after surgery, by Nov 20") rather than diagnoses, counseling content or family conflict, unless the church's policy and the leader opt in. Mainstream assistants supply the remaining patterns. Ephemeral or "incognito" modes neither read nor write memory, which matters for counseling-adjacent conversations. Users can pause memory or reset it. Sensitive categories are opt-in: Claude says "Enabling memory doesn't enable sensitive topics" ([Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11817273)). The documented pitfalls are just as instructive. In ChatGPT and Microsoft 365 Copilot, **deleting a chat does not delete memories derived from it**. Copilot memory generates no audit log entries, and admins "can't restrict what type of information is added to Copilot memory" ([OpenAI Memory FAQ, search excerpt](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8590148-memory-faq); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/copilot-personalization-memory)). This app should cascade deletes from conversation to memory, log every memory write, and give church admins a kill switch. Admins should not be able to read individual pastors' memories, which is the approach Claude takes for team owners.

The market makes these controls a differentiator rather than overhead. The only two church products that claim memory, Church Assistant by Verble and AlignedAI, document no view, edit or delete controls. Verble explicitly remembers "member details," the most sensitive category ([churchassistant.app](https://churchassistant.app/); [Aligned](https://aligned.church/blog/ai-ministry-assistant)). In the Barna/Pushpay study of 1,306 leaders, **83% are concerned about data privacy** and only 5% of churches have AI guidelines ([Barna](https://www.barna.com/research/church-leaders-ai-usage-concerns/)). A search excerpt of Barna's research reports that just 6% of pastors are comfortable using AI as a counseling tool ([Barna, search excerpt](https://www.barna.com/research/pastors-use-ai/)). Pushpay's own policy guidance describes staff disagreeing over whether AI-drafted pastoral follow-ups cross a line ([Pushpay blog](https://pushpay.com/blog/church-ai-policy-planning/)). A voice assistant that remembers, and shows leaders exactly what it remembers under rules the church sets, answers that concern directly. Because only 5% of churches have written AI guidelines, the app should ship those rules as sensible defaults.

## Conclusion

The five features and the voice assistant work best as one product, not six bets. The assistant is the delivery surface: the burnout radar becomes a 30-second drive-time briefing ("Sarah has served nine straight weeks across three teams"), care-load tracking captures a pastor's spoken note after a hospital visit, and the multilingual layer turns "text the Garcia family about Sunday" into a reviewed Spanish message. Each feature alone has a likely fast follower: Pushpay for burnout signals, ChMeetings for translation, Church Dashboards for care load, Planning Center for memory. No incumbent is close to the combination. The durable advantage is trust. The first church products to claim memory document no controls, so shipping learned memory with visible records, a pastoral incognito mode and policy-enforced rules about congregants turns the backup "privacy-governed AI" idea from a weak standalone into the product's spine.

Distribution should follow the incumbents' new openness rather than fight it. Building as a layer over Planning Center, MinistryPlatform, Rock and Breeze data through their 2026 MCP servers and APIs removes the switching fear that dominates small-church buying. The 2026–2027 migrations at Tithely, Vanco/ACS and Pushpay create natural moments to win churches. ChurchTrac is the awkward exception. Its closed API puts its claimed 14,000 churches out of reach of an overlay, so serving them requires CSV import or a standalone data model, and its $29–$119 all-included price sets the value bar every buyer will compare against. The biggest unknowns are commercial rather than technical. No willingness-to-pay data exists for any of these features, and three near-competitors (Church.IQ, Vitals and Church Dashboards) are worth a hands-on demo before positioning is final.
